## TL;DR

Loss aversion is the empirical finding that losses loom larger than equivalent gains. Across many experiments, a loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equivalent gain pleases, with loss-aversion coefficients typically falling between 1.5 and 2.5. Loss aversion is the asymmetric core of [[prospect-theory|Prospect Theory]] and is used to explain a cluster of downstream phenomena including the endowment effect, status quo bias, and the disposition effect.

## Key Facts

- Loss aversion is the most widely cited finding from prospect theory: losses are weighted more heavily than equivalent gains in how people evaluate outcomes. [prov:src-2026-04-10-kahneman-prospect-theory#sec:loss-aversion|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- The rule of thumb reported by Kahneman and Tversky, reproduced in dozens of subsequent studies, is that a loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equivalent gain pleases. [prov:src-2026-04-10-kahneman-prospect-theory#sec:loss-aversion|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- Empirical loss-aversion coefficients typically fall between 1.5 and 2.5, with about 2.0 as the consensus midpoint. [prov:src-2026-04-10-kahneman-prospect-theory#sec:loss-aversion|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- The endowment effect -- people demanding significantly more to give up an object than they would have paid to acquire it -- is usually explained as a downstream consequence of loss aversion, because ownership recodes disposal of the object as a loss. [prov:src-2026-04-10-kahneman-prospect-theory#sec:loss-aversion|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- Status quo bias is explained in the same way: any change from the current state is evaluated as a mixture of gains and losses, and because losses are weighted more heavily, the default option enjoys a built-in advantage. ## Detail

Loss aversion is the asymmetric weighting of gains and losses that sits at the geometric center of prospect theory. Kahneman and Tversky's experimental paradigm asked people to evaluate mixed gambles -- for example, a coin flip that would pay them one hundred dollars on heads and cost them X on tails -- and recorded the value of X at which participants became indifferent. Across many studies the typical answer clustered around fifty dollars, implying that the prospective loss needed to be roughly half the size of the prospective gain for the gamble to feel break-even. That ratio, expressed as a loss-aversion coefficient, usually falls between 1.5 and 2.5 depending on the task, the stakes, and the population.

The appeal of loss aversion is that it explains several phenomena that expected utility theory struggles with, using a single mechanism.

**Endowment effect.** When people own an object, they demand more to sell it than they would pay to acquire it. The classic experiments used coffee mugs, pens, and chocolate bars. Standard utility theory has no mechanism for this gap. Loss aversion offers one: once you own the mug, parting with it is coded as a loss, and losses are weighted more heavily than the prospective gain from selling it.

**Status quo bias.** People have a disproportionate preference for keeping things as they are, even when alternative arrangements would be strictly better on paper. Any change from the current state involves giving up some features and gaining others, so the total is evaluated as a mix of gains and losses. Because losses weigh more heavily, the default option enjoys a built-in advantage. This is why opt-in and opt-out defaults can produce large differences in outcomes such as organ donation enrollment and retirement plan participation, even when the mechanical effort required to switch is trivial.

**Disposition effect.** In financial markets, investors are reliably more willing to sell winning positions than losing ones, a pattern documented across retail and professional data sets. Loss aversion explains it: a paper loss is still abstract, but the moment a losing stock is sold, the loss becomes real and psychologically charged. The investor's reference point is the purchase price, and selling below it is experienced as a loss in a way that continuing to hold is not.

A separate and important observation is that loss aversion is difficult to train away. Even professional traders, statisticians, and decision analysts who know prospect theory continue to exhibit loss aversion in their own choices. The source article connects this to the dual-process framework: the immediate reaction to a prospective loss is characteristic of System 1 -- fast, effortless, and difficult to override -- while deliberate reasoning about probabilities and expected values is a System 2 activity. The knowledge lives in System 2 but the response is generated by System 1, and System 1 does not consult System 2 for permission before producing its verdict.
