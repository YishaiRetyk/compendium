## TL;DR

How humans make choices under uncertainty. Research by [[Daniel Kahneman]] and others reveals that decisions are shaped by [[Cognitive Biases]], the asymmetric weighting of gains and losses formalized in [[Prospect Theory]] and [[Loss Aversion]], and the interplay of [[System 1 vs System 2]] thinking. This overview synthesizes findings discussed across multiple wiki pages to present a unified picture of judgment and decision-making research.

- Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases research program demonstrated that human judgment departs from normative rational models in systematic, predictable ways -- not through random error but through identifiable cognitive shortcuts. - Synthesizing across the concepts discussed in this wiki, the dual-process framework provides the mechanistic explanation for why biases occur: System 1 generates quick intuitive judgments that System 2 often accepts without scrutiny, and the specific heuristics (anchoring, availability, representativeness) are properties of System 1's associative processing. [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:heuristics|synthesis] [epistemic:: inferred]
- Loss aversion and framing effects mean that how a decision is presented can change outcomes more than the underlying facts. - The field of judgment and decision-making research has moved beyond cataloging individual biases toward understanding their interactions, boundary conditions, and the conditions under which intuitive judgment performs well (as in naturalistic decision-making research). [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:introduction|synthesis] [epistemic:: inferred]
- In medical decision-making, a treatment described as having a ninety percent survival rate is chosen more often than the same treatment described as having a ten percent mortality rate, because the different reference points code the outcomes differently even when the underlying information is identical. - The "nudge" approach to public policy -- using default settings, framing choices, and simplified enrollment steps to guide decisions without restricting options -- draws directly on prospect theory's findings about reference points and loss aversion, and has been applied to retirement savings, organ donation, energy conservation, and tax compliance. ## Detail

The modern study of decision-making begins with the expected utility theory of von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944), which proposed that rational agents maximize the expected value of outcomes weighted by their probabilities. For decades, this normative model served as both a description and prescription for how people should choose. Kahneman and Tversky's research, starting in the early 1970s, systematically demonstrated that real human decisions violate expected utility theory in predictable ways.

The heuristics-and-biases framework, which grew out of Kahneman and Tversky's collaboration, identified three primary mental shortcuts -- representativeness, availability, and anchoring -- that people use to simplify complex judgments. While these heuristics are often adaptive, they produce systematic errors when applied outside their natural domain. The dual-process framework of System 1 and System 2, later popularized in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, provides the cognitive architecture that explains why these heuristics are so pervasive: they are products of System 1, which operates automatically and without conscious oversight.

The practical implications of this research extend across domains. In medicine, framing effects influence whether patients choose surgery or radiation therapy depending on whether outcomes are described in terms of survival rates or mortality rates. In law, anchoring effects influence sentencing decisions. In finance, loss aversion drives investors to hold losing stocks too long and sell winners too early. The common thread is that decision quality depends not just on information and intelligence but on the cognitive processes through which information is filtered. Improving decisions therefore requires designing environments and procedures that account for the systematic tendencies of the human mind -- a field now known as "choice architecture" or "nudging."

The "nudge" lineage draws directly on prospect theory rather than generically on bias research. Default settings work because any change from the current state gets evaluated as a mixture of gains and losses, and loss aversion gives the default a built-in advantage. Framing choices work because outcomes are coded relative to a reference point that the framing sets. Simplified enrollment steps work because friction in the opt-in direction compounds with the loss-aversion tilt toward the default. The specific mechanism -- that reference dependence and asymmetric weighting create predictable footholds for structural interventions -- is what makes nudge theory an application of prospect theory rather than an independent toolkit.
