## TL;DR

A first-person reflection on a week-long experiment in applying decision-making frameworks to real personal situations. The author reports that the frameworks only take hold when paired with the felt sense of a physical "flinch" in the moment before a decision seems inevitable, and that introspecting on reasoning after the fact is the wrong layer of intervention.

## Key Takeaways

Narrative takeaways — individual provenance markers live on the paragraph-level clusters in Extracted Claims below.

- The author's operational reframe is that cognitive frameworks work by training the noticing of a pre-decision "flinch," not by improving after-the-fact reasoning.
- Anchoring showed up in a real salary negotiation as indistinguishable from politeness, and was only interrupted by deleting a typed reply and walking away before countering.
- A productivity-app purchase was averted not by identifying a bias but by asking System 2 whether the underlying want could be explained; "can I explain the want?" emerged as a more tractable real-time test than "is this a bias?"
- A pre-mortem on a stalled eight-month side project surfaced the author's own loss-aversion rationale for continuing — the project was kept running to avoid crystallizing the time as a loss.
- Personal decision fatigue kicks in above roughly four-to-five meaningful decisions per day; the practical response is batching, pre-commitment, and a four-decision daily cap.
- Three concrete action items were committed to for the next two weeks: pre-mortems on long-running "definitely going to do" projects, the articulate-the-want test before purchases, and a four-decision daily cap.

## Extracted Claims

- **Morning reflection and the "flinch" reframe.** After years of reading about biases with no behavioral change, the author spent a week deliberately running three specific frameworks (anchoring, System 1 vs System 2, loss aversion) on three real decisions. The only result worth keeping was catching three in-the-moment flinches — the physical sensation that precedes rationalization — and discovering that the flinch is the tell, not the reasoning. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:1|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- **Salary negotiation and anchoring as politeness.** A contract-rate counterparty opened about twenty percent below the author's private fair number. The author's first reflex was to counter with a middle number — to accept the opening as the frame. Remembering the anchoring material, the author deleted the draft, walked away, and returned with a counter-offer slightly above their fair number to explicitly reset the anchor. The experiential observation was that anchoring and politeness occupy the same internal slot and are not felt as distinct. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:2|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- **The autopilot purchase and the "explain the want" test.** The author was fifteen seconds from buying a productivity app, then noticed zero memory of deciding to want it — the desire had appeared fully formed. Instead of trying to identify a bias, the author asked System 2 "what problem am I solving?" and could not answer, then closed the tab. The author's proposed personal heuristic is "can I explain the want?", which they find more tractable than real-time bias identification. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:3|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- **Pre-mortem on a stalled eight-month project and loss-aversion self-diagnosis.** The author ran a pre-mortem on a side project they had been refusing to quit. The failure reasons they wrote down were reasons they already knew; what the exercise actually surfaced was that they were continuing the project to avoid crystallizing the eight months as a loss. The project had not been shut down at the time of writing, but the stated reason for continuing had been recognized as different from the told reason. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:4|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- **Personal decision fatigue threshold.** The author reports a personal threshold of roughly four-to-five meaningful decisions per day before quality drops — reaching for defaults, postponing, snapping at small things. On one Wednesday with nine stacked decisions the author observed making choices they would not endorse in someone else. The suggested structural move is batching low-stakes decisions, pre-committing, and reserving deliberation slots for decisions that actually matter. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:5|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- **Three action items the author committed to.** (1) Run a five-minute pre-mortem on anything called "definitely going to do" for more than a month — targeting sunk projects, not fresh ones. (2) Before any purchase over a small threshold, articulate the problem being solved in one sentence out loud; if unable, do not buy. (3) Cap meaningful decisions at four per day; anything over four is deferred or delegated to a pre-committed default. The novelty is framing these as interventions on the author's own behavior rather than as concepts read about. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:6|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- **Meta-observation: introspect on the flinch, not on the reasoning.** The author's closing synthesis is that frameworks only work when paired with the felt sense of a pre-decision flinch, because by the time reasoning is happening System 1 has already handed System 2 a pre-formed conclusion to rationalize. The proposed training target is noticing the flinch earlier, and the author plans to keep a running log of flinches. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:7|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
