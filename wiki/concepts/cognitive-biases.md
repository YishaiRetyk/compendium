## TL;DR

Systematic patterns of deviation from rationality in judgment. First cataloged by [[Daniel Kahneman]] and [[Amos Tversky]] in the 1970s, cognitive biases arise from the mind's reliance on mental shortcuts (heuristics) that are efficient but systematically error-prone. They are central to [[Decision Making]] research. A second family of biases -- including the endowment effect, status quo bias, and the disposition effect -- is explained downstream of [[Loss Aversion]] and [[Prospect Theory]] rather than by heuristic shortcuts.

## Key Facts

- Anchoring bias causes people to rely disproportionately on the first piece of information encountered when making judgments. In experiments, arbitrary starting numbers significantly influenced estimates even when participants knew the anchor was random. [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:heuristics|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- The availability heuristic leads people to judge the frequency or likelihood of events based on how easily examples come to mind, causing overestimation of dramatic risks like plane crashes and underestimation of common ones like diabetes. [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:availability|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- Representativeness bias causes people to judge probabilities by how closely something matches a prototype, leading to neglect of base rates and the conjunction fallacy (e.g., the Linda problem). [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:representativeness|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- Most cognitive biases arise from System 1's reliance on heuristic shortcuts -- the speed and automaticity that make System 1 efficient are the same properties that produce systematic errors. This connection is implied throughout the source but is not stated as a single unified proposition. [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:two-systems|synthesis] [epistemic:: inferred]
- Evidence is mixed on whether anchoring effects observed in laboratory settings translate with the same magnitude to real-world high-stakes decisions such as judicial sentencing or salary negotiations. Some researchers question whether the controlled conditions of bias experiments capture the complexity of naturalistic decision-making environments. [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:heuristics|background] [epistemic:: tentative]
- Early estimates from 1970s studies suggested that the availability heuristic accounts for a large share of probability judgment errors. This finding dates from the original Tversky and Kahneman research program; subsequent meta-analyses and replication efforts have revised these estimates downward, and some specific availability effects have proven difficult to replicate in pre-registered studies. [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:availability|background] [epistemic:: stale]
- Loss aversion -- the finding that losses are felt roughly twice as strongly as equivalent gains -- is one of the most influential results in behavioral economics and underpins prospect theory. [prov:src-2026-04-09-thinking-fast-and-slow-part1#sec:prospect-theory|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- The endowment effect -- the tendency for people to demand significantly more to give up an object than they would have paid to acquire it -- is usually explained as a downstream consequence of loss aversion: ownership recodes disposal as a loss, and losses are weighted more heavily than the prospective gain from selling. [prov:src-2026-04-10-kahneman-prospect-theory#sec:loss-aversion|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- Status quo bias -- the disproportionate preference for keeping things as they are -- is explained by the same loss-aversion mechanism: any change from the current state is evaluated as a mixture of gains and losses, and because losses weigh more heavily, the default option enjoys a built-in advantage. [prov:src-2026-04-10-kahneman-prospect-theory#sec:loss-aversion|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- The disposition effect in financial markets -- investors being more willing to sell winning positions than losing ones -- is attributed to loss aversion: realizing a loss crystallizes a psychological cost that a paper loss does not. [prov:src-2026-04-10-kahneman-prospect-theory#sec:applications|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]

## Detail

The study of cognitive biases began in the early 1970s when Kahneman and Tversky published a series of papers demonstrating that human judgment systematically departs from the predictions of probability theory and expected utility. Rather than being random, these errors follow predictable patterns that can be traced to specific mental shortcuts -- heuristics -- that the mind uses to simplify complex problems.

The heuristics-and-biases research program identified three core heuristics: representativeness (judging by similarity to a prototype), availability (judging by ease of recall), and anchoring (adjusting insufficiently from an initial value). Each heuristic is useful in many everyday situations but leads to characteristic errors when applied in domains where it does not fit. The dual-process framework presented in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* locates these heuristics in [[System 1 vs System 2]] -- the fast, automatic system that processes information without deliberate effort.

The field of cognitive bias research has faced scrutiny in recent years due to the broader replication crisis in psychology. While the core heuristics have generally replicated well, the precise magnitude and boundary conditions of specific biases remain subjects of active investigation. Kahneman himself acknowledged some of these concerns in later work, noting that the original effect sizes for certain priming-related biases were likely inflated. This ongoing refinement is a normal part of science, but it means that claims about specific bias magnitudes should be treated with appropriate caution.

Not every cognitive bias catalogued in the literature is a heuristic bias in the Tversky-Kahneman sense. A second family of biases -- the endowment effect, status quo bias, and the disposition effect -- is not derived from shortcut heuristics at all, but from the asymmetric weighting of gains and losses formalized in prospect theory. The endowment effect shows up in laboratory trades of coffee mugs and pens, where people who have just been given an object demand significantly more to part with it than they would have paid to acquire it. Status quo bias shows up in large-scale policy settings: opt-in versus opt-out defaults produce enormous differences in organ donation enrollment and retirement plan participation even when the mechanical effort to switch is trivial. The disposition effect shows up in financial market data, where investors hold onto losing stocks and prematurely sell winners. All three are usually explained through loss aversion rather than through anchoring, availability, or representativeness -- see the loss aversion page for the dedicated treatment.
