## TL;DR

Personal, experiential observations about what it takes to actually apply cognitive decision-making frameworks — anchoring, dual-process, loss aversion, pre-mortems, decision fatigue — to real-life choices. The organizing synthesis from practice is that frameworks do not work by improving after-the-fact reasoning; they work by training the ability to notice a pre-decision physical "flinch" in the moment before a decision feels inevitable. This page holds the author's own lived experiments and committed action items, not general-knowledge content about the underlying concepts.

## Key Facts

- The active intervention point is the pre-decision flinch, not the reasoning that follows it; introspecting on reasoning is downstream of System 1 having already produced a conclusion. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:1|direct] [epistemic:: sourced] [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:7|synthesis] [epistemic:: inferred]
- Anchoring in a real negotiation felt subjectively identical to politeness — the pull to accept the counterparty's opening number as the frame registered as social courtesy, not as a bias. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:2|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- "Can I explain the want?" is more operationally useful in the moment than "is this a bias?" for interrupting autopilot purchases. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:3|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]
- A pre-mortem on a stalled side project surfaced a loss-aversion-based reason for continuing (avoiding crystallizing the prior months as a loss) that was different from the told reason (belief the project would work). [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:4|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]

## Detail

### Anchoring in a real negotiation

In a salary-negotiation conversation the counterparty opened with a number about twenty percent below the author's private fair number. The immediate reflex was to counter with a middle number — to accept the opening as the reference frame and negotiate within it. The interrupt that broke this was not a clever cognitive move; it was deleting the typed draft, physically walking away, then returning and counter-offering slightly above the fair number to deliberately reset the anchor. The experiential lesson was that the pull to accept the counterparty's frame did not feel like a bias — it felt like politeness, and politeness and anchoring appear to occupy the same internal slot. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:2|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]

### System 1 / System 2 and the "explain the want" test

The author was fifteen seconds from buying a productivity app with the checkout page open when they noticed zero memory of having decided to want the app. The desire had appeared fully formed between seeing the third recommendation and opening the checkout page, which is a signature of System 1 running unopposed. Rather than trying to identify a specific bias, the author asked a narrower question: "Is there a problem I am trying to solve, and if so what is it?" Being unable to answer ended the purchase. The proposed personal heuristic, generalized out of this incident, is the "explain the want" test — substantially more tractable in real time than the "is this a bias" test, which the author finds difficult to run under time pressure. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:3|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]

### Loss aversion, pre-mortems, and the stalled side project

The harder case from the week was an eight-month side project the author had been refusing to quit. A five-minute pre-mortem — imagining the project having failed at six months and writing down the reasons — did not surface any new failure modes; the reasons were all already known. What it did surface was the author's real motivation for continuing: shutting the project down would crystallize the eight months as a loss, whereas leaving it running kept the loss on paper. This is a textbook loss-aversion pattern observed in the author's own behavior. The immediate outcome is not that the project was shut down — it was not — but that the author separated the told reason for continuing (belief the project would work) from the actual reason (loss aversion on sunk time), which is the first condition for any future shutdown to be deliberate. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:4|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]

The pre-mortem's surprising strength here was that it targeted an already-sunk project, not a freshly considered one. The author's derived rule for when to run the technique is therefore "anything I have been calling 'definitely going to do' for more than a month" — i.e., the test is most valuable on sunk rather than fresh commitments. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:6|synthesis] [epistemic:: inferred]

### Decision fatigue threshold and structural response

Separate from the single-decision cases, the author observed a personal threshold around four-to-five meaningful decisions per day, above which decision quality noticeably drops — reaching for defaults, postponing, and snapping at small things. On one Wednesday with nine stacked decisions the author reports making choices they would not stand behind if they observed someone else making them. The practical response suggested by the general decision-fatigue literature — batching low-stakes decisions, pre-committing where possible, and reserving explicit deliberation slots for the decisions that actually matter — was not yet systematized at the time of writing, but its intended shape was clear: a small number of pre-made defaults for recurring choices, and deliberation reserved for non-recurring ones. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:5|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]

### Committed action items

For a two-week personal experiment starting 2026-04-10, the author committed to three concrete changes. First, run a five-minute pre-mortem on anything that has been labeled "definitely going to do" for more than a month — the pre-mortem is most useful on sunk projects, not freshly-considered ones. Second, before any purchase above a small threshold, force articulation of the problem being solved in one sentence, out loud; if the articulation fails, the purchase does not happen. Third, cap meaningful decisions at four per day, with anything over four deferred to the next day or delegated to a pre-committed default. The author's framing is that none of these ideas are novel — what is novel is treating them as interventions on their own behavior rather than as read-about concepts. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:6|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]

### Meta-observation: the flinch is the tell

The week's integrating observation is that decision-frameworks operate at a different layer than the author previously assumed. The old failed approach — "be less biased by reasoning more carefully" — targeted the wrong moment, because by the time reasoning is in progress System 1 has already handed System 2 a pre-formed conclusion to rationalize. The working approach targets the pre-decision flinch instead: a quiet physical sensation in the instant before a decision starts to feel inevitable. All three successful interrupts from the week (the deleted salary reply, the closed checkout tab, the surfaced sunk-cost rationale) shared this same felt quality. The author's explicit training target going forward is noticing the flinch earlier, and the concrete next artifact is a running log of flinches. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:7|direct] [epistemic:: sourced]

Pulling the threads together: the same flinch-level intervention point explains why the anchoring interrupt required a physical walk (not an argument), why the "explain the want" test works when the "is this a bias" test does not (it reaches for a pre-reasoning layer), and why the pre-mortem worked on the stalled project only when it targeted the felt reason for continuing rather than the told reason. All four observations are the same claim from different angles. [prov:src-2026-04-10-personal-decision-journal#para:1|synthesis] [epistemic:: inferred]
